## Ch 1 — Hook (0:00, ~40s)
[hook1–3: chalky loaf push-in]

Your soap has a white crust. Or it's still soft after two days. Or there are orange spots, or little beads of water, or it just crumbled when you cut it.

Before you throw that batch in the bin, stop. Most soap problems look far worse than they are. Some are purely cosmetic and you can sell those bars. A few are genuinely unsafe, and you need to know the difference.

In this video I'll go through ten of the most common cold process soap problems. For each one I'll cover what it looks like, why it happens, whether the soap is still usable, and how to stop it happening again. The chapters are in the description, so jump straight to your problem.

## Ch 2 — Soda ash (~1:15)
[sodaash1–3, B-roll pour]

Problem one: soda ash. That's a white, powdery, sometimes crystal-like film on the top of your bars. It usually shows up a day or two after you pour.

Soda ash forms when lye near the surface, before it has fully reacted with the oils, meets carbon dioxide in the air. The result is sodium carbonate sitting on the surface. It's not dangerous and it doesn't mean your soap is lye heavy. It's cosmetic.

To fix it, a garment steamer or kettle steam held over the bar for a few seconds will often clear it. You can also rinse the bar under warm water, or plane a thin layer off the top with a vegetable peeler or soap planer.

To prevent it, there are four things that help. Spritz the top with ninety-nine per cent isopropyl alcohol straight after pouring. Cover the mold with cling film pressed onto the surface, or a lid. Use a water discount, meaning less water in your recipe, so the soap sets up faster. And pour at a slightly thicker trace, not while the batter is still very fluid.

## Ch 3 — Seized soap (~1:15)
[seize1–3]

Problem two: seizing. You add your fragrance, and within seconds the batter goes from custard to lumpy mashed potato. It's the most panic-inducing moment in soap making.

The usual cause is a fragrance oil. Some fragrances, especially floral, spice and some vanilla-type scents, speed up the reaction dramatically. Working too hot can make it worse.

The fix is simple: don't stop. Put down the stick blender, grab a spatula and work fast. Stir the fragrance through by hand, then glop the batter into the mold and bang the mold on the bench to knock out air pockets. The top will be rustic, but the soap is usually perfectly fine once it has cured.

To prevent it, read the supplier's notes on every fragrance before you use it. Many list whether a fragrance accelerates trace. Test any new fragrance in a small batch first. Add fragrance at a thin trace and stir it in by hand rather than blending. And soap a little cooler.

## Ch 4 — Soap still soft after two days (~1:30)
[soft1–3, B-roll oils]

Problem three, and one I see asked constantly in soap groups: "It's been two days and my soap is still soft. Is it ruined?"

Usually, no. Soft soap after two days is normal for a lot of recipes. Recipes high in soft oils, like olive oil, can take a week or longer to firm up enough to unmold. A pure olive oil castile bar is famously slow.

Other causes are too much water in the recipe, a high superfat, a cold room, or a batch that didn't go through gel phase.

So first, give it more time. Leave it in the mold another three to five days somewhere warm.

Here's the important part. If it's soft and also greasy, with pockets of liquid oil, or it's separating in the mold, that points to a measurement problem. Usually that means too little lye, or lye that has absorbed moisture from the air because the container wasn't sealed. Put your exact recipe back through a lye calculator and check your weights. Don't guess.

To prevent it: use a water discount, add sodium lactate to your lye water for a harder bar, and balance soft oils with hard oils and butters.

## Ch 5 — Cracked tops and overheating (~1:15)
[crack1–3, B-roll mold]

Problem four: a crack down the middle of the loaf, sometimes with a domed top or liquid bubbling up through it. Soap makers call it a volcano.

This is overheating. Saponification makes heat, and some ingredients add a lot more: honey, milks, sugars, beer, and certain fragrances. Add a warm room and a well-insulated mold and the soap can get too hot.

A cosmetic crack is usually fine. Let it cure, then trim the top. If liquid came up through the crack, wear gloves. Don't touch it with bare skin, and wait. Often it's reabsorbed within a day. If there are still wet pockets after a couple of days, treat the batch as suspect and don't sell it.

To prevent it: soap cooler, don't insulate recipes with milk, honey or sugar, and for those recipes put the mold in the fridge or freezer for the first day.

## Ch 6 — Glycerin rivers (~1:00)
[rivers1–3]

Problem five: glycerin rivers. These are translucent, vein-like streaks running through coloured soap, often most visible where titanium dioxide was used.

They're cosmetic. The soap is completely safe to use and sell. Some makers even like the effect.

They're linked to excess heat, a partial gel, and higher water amounts, especially when titanium dioxide is dispersed in water.

To prevent them: use a water discount, disperse titanium dioxide in a little oil instead of water, and either keep the soap consistently cool or fully insulate it so it gels evenly all the way through. A half-and-half gel is when rivers show up most.

## Ch 7 — Crumbly or brittle soap (~1:15)
[crumble1–3, B-roll cut]

Problem six: the soap crumbles or chips when you cut it.

There are two very different causes, and you need to know which one you have.

The harmless one: you cut too late. Recipes high in coconut oil, palm or stearic acid get very hard very fast. Leave them a few days and they'll chip under the knife or wire. The fix for next time is to cut within about twenty-four hours.

The serious one: the soap is lye heavy. Signs include white, hard, chalky lumps inside the bar, a bar that feels harsh or burning on skin, and the other lye-heavy signs I'll cover in problem ten. That comes from a measuring mistake, a wrong setting in the calculator, or false trace from butters that weren't fully melted.

If you suspect lye heavy, don't use it and don't sell it. Re-check the recipe in a calculator before doing anything else.

## Ch 8 — Sweating soap (~1:00)
[sweat1–3, B-roll curing]

Problem seven: sweating. Your bars are covered in tiny beads of liquid, like condensation.

That's glycerin doing its job. Glycerin is a natural by-product of saponification, and it pulls moisture out of humid air. It's harmless and very common in damp climates, and Ireland and the UK definitely count.

Wipe the bars dry and move them somewhere with more airflow. A fan or a dehumidifier in the curing room makes a big difference.

Don't wrap bars in plastic while they're still curing, because that traps moisture. Once fully cured, wrap them in something breathable or shrink wrap them for sale, and store them away from bathrooms and kitchens.

## Ch 9 — DOS: dreaded orange spots (~1:15)
[dos1–3]

Problem eight is known as DOS, dreaded orange spots. Small orange or brown spots show up on bars, often weeks or months later, and sometimes with an old, crayon-like smell.

DOS is rancidity. The free oils left in the bar are oxidising.

The common causes: oils that were already old or near their best-before date, a high percentage of oils like sunflower, grapeseed or hemp that go rancid easily, a very high superfat, tap water with dissolved metals, and humid or warm storage.

You can't reverse it. A couple of small spots can be cut out and the bar kept for personal use. If the spots are widespread or the bar smells off, don't sell it.

To prevent it: use fresh oils, keep fast-oxidising oils as a small part of the recipe, stick to a sensible superfat, use distilled water, add an antioxidant like rosemary oleoresin extract to your oils, and cure and store in a cool, dry, dark place.

## Ch 10 — Scent fading (~1:15)
[scent1–3, B-roll pour]

Problem nine: your soap smelled amazing on day one, and by the end of the cure you can barely smell it.

Scent fade is normal, but some scents fade much faster than others. Citrus essential oils are the worst for it. Heat from gel phase can also drive off lighter notes.

Here's how to make scent last. Blend light top notes with heavier base notes, which anchor the scent. Some makers use concentrated, or "folded", citrus oils, which hold up better than standard ones. Adding clay to your fragrance before mixing it in can help anchor it. Store cured bars wrapped, in a cool, dark place.

One rule matters more than any of those. Never just add more and more fragrance to fix fading. Every essential oil and fragrance oil has a maximum safe usage rate for soap. Check your supplier's IFRA documentation and stay within it. For UK and EU sellers, your safety assessment will be based on it.

## Ch 11 — Is my soap safe? (~1:30)
[safety1–3, B-roll lye]

Problem ten, and the most important one: is this soap safe to use?

Everything so far has mostly been cosmetic. What actually makes soap unsafe is excess lye.

These are the warning signs of a lye-heavy bar:
- Pockets of clear liquid that don't reabsorb after a day or two. Treat that liquid as caustic and handle it with gloves.
- White, hard, chalky lumps inside the bar, not just on the surface.
- Soap that stings, burns or leaves skin red.
- A bar that crumbles and feels harsh right through, not just at the cut edges.

The first step is always the same: go back to your recipe. Put the exact oils and weights through a lye calculator and compare the lye amount to what you actually weighed. Check that the calculator was set to sodium hydroxide for bar soap, not potassium hydroxide, which is for liquid soap. And check your scale is accurate.

If the numbers show too much lye, don't use it and don't sell it. If you can't confirm what went wrong, don't take the risk. A batch of soap is cheap compared to someone's skin, or your reputation.

## Ch 12 — Prevention: it starts with the recipe (~1:30)
[prevent1–3, laptop/calculator screen recording]

Look back over those ten problems. Most of them trace back to the same few causes: the recipe, the water amount, the temperature, and how carefully everything was weighed.

So here are five habits that will prevent most of what we've covered.

One. Run every recipe through a lye calculator, every time, even recipes you've made before. It takes a minute.

Two. Weigh everything in grams on a digital scale. Never use cups or volume.

Three. Keep your lye sealed and your oils fresh, and write the purchase date on every container.

Four. Test every new fragrance in a small batch before a full loaf.

Five. Keep a record of every batch: the recipe, temperatures, water amount, fragrance, and what happened. If you sell soap in the UK or EU, you need batch records anyway, and they turn every mistake into a lesson instead of a mystery.
