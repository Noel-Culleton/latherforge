Every soap calculator has a setting called superfat, and almost every recipe sets it to five percent. But what does it actually do, and should you ever change it?

In this video I'll explain superfat in plain English, show you exactly what it does to your lye amount, and when it makes sense to go higher or lower.

## Ch 2: What superfat is
[sf_oil1, sf_bar1, broll_pour1]

*On screen:* SPARE OIL · A little oil / left unsaponified

Superfat is simply spare oil.

When you make soap, lye reacts with oil and turns it into soap. If you used exactly enough lye to turn every drop of oil into soap, that would be zero percent superfat.

Instead, we use slightly less lye than that. A small percentage of the oil stays as oil in the finished bar. That's the superfat.

## Ch 3: Why we use it
[sf_scale1, sf_hands1, broll_calc1]

*On screen:* WHY WE DO IT · Safety margin / + a milder bar

There are two reasons.

The first is safety. SAP values are averages. Real oils vary a little from batch to batch, and kitchen scales aren't perfect. A superfat gives you a margin, so small differences don't leave free lye in the bar.

The second is feel. That little bit of spare oil makes the bar feel milder and more conditioning.

## Ch 4: What it does to your lye
[sf_calc1, broll_calc1, sf_beads1]

*On screen:* WHAT IT DOES TO LYE · 0% = 148 g · 5% ≈ 141 g / 10% ≈ 133 g

Here's what it does in practice. Take our beginner recipe at one kilo of oils.

At zero percent superfat, the calculator says 148 grams of sodium hydroxide. At five percent, it's about 141 grams. At ten percent, about 133 grams.

So superfat doesn't add oil to your recipe. It reduces the lye. That's why you set it in the calculator and let it do the maths, rather than adding extra oil after you've measured.

## Ch 5: Why 5% works
[sf_cure1, broll_cut1, broll_cure1]

*On screen:* WHY 5%? · Enough margin / Still a firm bar

Why five percent? It's a balance.

It's enough of a margin for normal variation in oils and scales, and it still gives a firm, long-lasting bar with good lather. That's why it's the default in our free calculator, and the usual starting point for most cold process recipes.

## Ch 6: When to go higher or lower
[sf_salt1, sf_dos1, broll_calc1]

*On screen:* HIGHER OR LOWER? · Salt bars: higher / Too high: soft, rancid

When would you change it?

Some recipes go higher on purpose. Salt bars and very high coconut oil soaps often use a superfat of fifteen to twenty percent, because those recipes can feel drying otherwise.

Go too high in an ordinary recipe and you'll get a softer bar that doesn't last as long, and spare oil that can go rancid and cause orange spots.

Going lower than five percent leaves less margin for error. If you're starting out, stay at five, and only change it for a recipe that's designed for it.
