*On screen:* WHITE STUFF ON YOUR SOAP? · It's called soda ash.

You unmould a beautiful loaf of soap, and the top is covered in a dull, white, chalky film. Sometimes it's a light dusting. Sometimes it looks like frost, with little feathery crystals.

That's soda ash, and it's one of the most common things new soap makers ask about.

In this video I'll explain what it actually is, whether your soap is safe, how to get rid of it, and four simple ways to stop it forming in the first place.

## Ch 2: What soda ash is
[ash_macro1, ash_pour1, broll_mould1]

*On screen:* WHAT IS SODA ASH? · Lye + air / = sodium carbonate

Soda ash is sodium carbonate. It forms when lye at the surface of fresh soap meets carbon dioxide in the air, before that lye has had a chance to react with the oils.

That's why it's almost always on the top of the loaf, or on any surface that was exposed to air while the soap was still soft. The inside of the bar is usually clear.

The key point: soda ash is cosmetic. Once your soap has cured, it doesn't make the bar unsafe or harsh. It just doesn't look as nice.

## Ch 3: What makes it worse
[ash_thintrace1, ash_cool1, broll_pour1]

*On screen:* WHAT MAKES IT WORSE · Cool soap · thin trace / Air on the surface

A few things make soda ash more likely.

Soaping cool, so the batter stays soft for longer. Pouring at very thin trace. A recipe that's slow to firm up, like high olive oil soaps. And leaving the top of the mould open to the air in a cold or draughty room.

None of these are mistakes as such. They just give the lye at the surface more time to meet the air.

## Ch 4: Four ways to prevent it
[ash_spray1, ash_cover1, broll_mould1]

*On screen:* 4 WAYS TO STOP IT · Spray · cover / Insulate · thicker trace

Here are four ways to prevent it.

One: spray the top with ninety-nine percent isopropyl alcohol straight after pouring. It forms a thin barrier over the surface. Alcohol is flammable, so keep it well away from any flame or hob.

Two: cover the mould. Plastic wrap laid right on the surface, or a lid or a sheet of cardboard, keeps the air off.

Three: keep the soap warm. A little insulation helps the soap go through gel phase and harden faster, which gives soda ash less time to form. Don't overdo it with milk or honey recipes, because they can overheat.

Four: pour at a slightly thicker trace, so the surface firms up sooner.

## Ch 5: How to remove soda ash
[ash_steam1, ash_plane1, broll_cut1]

*On screen:* HOW TO REMOVE IT · Steam · plane / Rinse with gloves on

Already got soda ash? You have a few options.

Steam is the most popular. A clothes steamer, or holding the bar carefully over kettle steam, melts the ash away and leaves a glossy surface. Keep your fingers clear of the steam.

You can also plane the top off with a vegetable peeler or a knife, or rinse the bar under running water and rub it gently.

Wait until the soap has been unmoulded for at least a day or two, and wear gloves, because fresh soap is still caustic. Then let it dry and cure as normal.

## Ch 6: Ash or something else?
[ash_compare1, broll_calc1, broll_cure1]

*On screen:* ASH OR SOMETHING ELSE? · Powdery and dry = ash / Wet or oily = check recipe

One last check. Soda ash is dry and powdery or crystalline, and it sits on the surface.

If instead you see wet beads of liquid, oily pockets, or white spots deep inside the bar that are hard and gritty, that's a different problem. It can point to lye that didn't mix in properly, or a measuring mistake.

In that case, don't use the soap on skin until you've re-checked your recipe in a soap calculator. When in doubt, throw it out.
