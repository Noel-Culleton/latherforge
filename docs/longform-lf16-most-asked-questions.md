## Ch 2: Can you make soap without lye? (~1:15)
[q1_lye, q1_mp]

This is the most common question, and the honest answer is no. All real soap is made with lye.

Soap is what you get when fats and oils react with a strong alkali. For bar soap that alkali is sodium hydroxide. For liquid soap it's potassium hydroxide. That chemical reaction is called saponification, and without it you just have oil.

You'll see videos claiming you can use baking soda instead. You can't. Baking soda is far too weak to turn oil into soap.

So what about "no-lye" soap kits? Those use melt and pour base. The manufacturer already made that base with lye, and the reaction is finished before it reaches you. You're melting and re-pouring soap that already exists. It's a great way to start, and you never handle lye yourself, but the lye was still part of the process.

## Ch 3: Is homemade soap safe? (~1:15)
[q2_safe, broll_cure]

Yes, if it's made properly. The worry is that lye is caustic, and that's true while it's raw. But when soap is made correctly, the lye is used up completely in the reaction with the oils. A well-made, cured bar contains no free lye.

Two things make that happen.

First, an accurate recipe. Every oil needs a specific amount of lye, so you run your recipe through a lye calculator and weigh everything in grams.

Second, a superfat. Most soap makers use about 5%. That means a little more oil than the lye can react with, so there's always a safety margin of oil, never leftover lye.

Where people get into trouble is guessing amounts, using volume instead of weight, or skipping the calculator. Do those three things right and handmade soap is as safe as anything you buy.

## Ch 4: What do you need to get started? (~1:20)
[q3_kit, safety1]

Less than you might think. Here's the basic kit:

- A digital scale that weighs in grams. This is the most important tool you'll buy.
- A stick blender.
- A stainless steel pot for melting oils.
- A heat-safe plastic jug for your lye solution. Look for number 5 polypropylene.
- A silicone loaf mold.
- Nitrile gloves, safety goggles and long sleeves.

Never use aluminium. Lye reacts with it.

For ingredients you need lye, distilled water and your oils.

The two lye safety rules that matter most: always add the lye to the water, never water to lye. And mix it somewhere well ventilated, because the solution gets very hot and gives off fumes for the first minute or so.

## Ch 5: What's the easiest first recipe? (~1:15)
[q4_oils, broll_oils, broll_blend]

Keep your first batch simple, unscented and uncoloured, so you can learn how soap behaves.

A balanced beginner recipe looks something like this:
- 45% olive oil, for gentleness
- 25% coconut oil, for big bubbles and cleansing
- 20% shea butter, for a hard, creamy bar
- 5% castor oil, to boost the lather
- 5% cocoa butter, for extra hardness

Those are percentages, not amounts. Put them into a free soap calculator with your batch size and a 5% superfat, and it gives you the exact lye and water weights.

Then make one loaf, about a kilo of oils, and nothing more. Your first batch is a practice run. Save the swirls and fragrances for batch two.

## Ch 6: Cold process, hot process or melt and pour? (~1:30)
[q5_cp, q5_hp, q1_mp]

There are three main methods.

**Melt and pour** is the simplest. You buy a ready-made soap base, melt it, add colour and scent, and pour it into a mold. It's usable as soon as it sets. There's no lye handling and it's great with kids, but you can't control the ingredients in the base.

**Cold process** is what most handmade soap makers use. You mix your lye solution and oils, blend to trace (that's when the batter thickens like custard), and pour it into a mold. You get full control over every ingredient, plus smooth tops and swirls. The catch is that it needs weeks to cure.

**Hot process** starts the same way, but then you cook the batter, usually in a slow cooker, until saponification is complete. It looks like mashed potato and gives a rustic, textured bar. You can use it sooner than cold process, though many makers still let it cure for a week or two so it's harder and lasts longer.

If you want full control, go cold process. If you want it quick and simple, melt and pour.

## Ch 7: How long does soap need to cure? (~1:15)
[q6_cure, broll_cure]

Cold process soap usually needs 4 to 6 weeks.

During that time, the water evaporates, the bar gets harder, and it becomes milder and longer-lasting in the shower. Soap you use after one week will feel soft and melt away quickly.

Some recipes take longer. A bar made from 100% olive oil can take 6 months or more to reach its best.

To cure soap, stand the bars on a rack with space between them, somewhere cool, dry and airy, out of direct sun. Turn them now and then. Don't wrap them in plastic until they're fully cured.

This is also why timing matters if you sell. Soap you want for Christmas markets has to be made in October, not December.

## Ch 8: How do you make goat milk soap? (~1:30)
[q7_goat, broll_pour]

Goat milk soap is the most-searched soap recipe by name, and it's easy to get wrong.

The problem is heat. Milk contains sugars, and when you add lye directly to fresh milk, it gets hot enough to scorch. You end up with orange batter and a strong ammonia smell.

The fix is to freeze the milk first. Pour it into ice cube trays and freeze it solid. Then sprinkle the lye over the frozen cubes slowly, a little at a time, stirring as they melt. Standing the jug in a bowl of iced water helps even more. Aim for a pale cream colour.

Some ammonia smell is normal and fades during cure.

Two more tips. Soap at cooler temperatures. And don't wrap the mold in towels, because milk soaps can overheat and crack. Some makers even pop the mold in the fridge for the first day.

## Ch 9: Castile soap and tallow soap (~1:30)
[q8_castile, q8_tallow]

Two of the most-searched soap styles are complete opposites.

**Castile soap** is made with 100% olive oil. It's famously gentle, but it behaves differently. It takes a long time to reach trace, it starts out soft, and the lather is low and a little slippery rather than bubbly. It also needs that long cure, often 6 months or more. If you want the gentleness without the wait, try a "bastile": mostly olive oil with a little coconut and castor.

**Tallow soap** goes the other way. Tallow is rendered beef fat, and it makes a very hard, long-lasting bar with a creamy, stable lather. It's the traditional soap our great-grandparents made, and it's having a real comeback. Most tallow recipes add some coconut oil for bigger bubbles. Make sure the tallow is properly rendered and strained, and run it through a lye calculator like any other oil.

## Ch 10: How do you make liquid soap? (~1:15)
[q9_liquid]

Here's where a lot of people go wrong: you can't make real liquid soap just by melting down bars, and you can't make it with sodium hydroxide.

Liquid soap uses potassium hydroxide, often called KOH, instead. The process is closer to hot process. You cook the oils and potassium hydroxide solution, usually for a few hours, until it becomes a thick, translucent paste. Then you dilute that paste with hot water to the thickness you want.

Make sure your lye calculator is set to potassium hydroxide, not sodium hydroxide, because the amounts are different. Get that wrong and the soap won't be safe.

It takes more time than bar soap, but one batch of paste makes a lot of liquid soap.

## Ch 11: Can you sell your soap and make money? (~1:45)
[q10_market, q10_pack]

Yes. Lots of people turn soap making into a real side business, especially around Christmas markets. But there are two things you need to sort out first.

**The rules.** In the UK and the EU, soap sold to the public is a cosmetic. Before you sell, each recipe needs a safety assessment by a qualified assessor. You need a Responsible Person based in the UK or EU, which is usually you. Each product has to be notified: on SCPN in the UK, or CPNP in the EU. And your labels need the ingredients in INCI names, a batch number, your address and the weight.

In the US, it depends what you claim. Simple soap sold just as soap is treated differently from soap marketed with cosmetic claims like "moisturising", which brings in FDA cosmetic rules.

This isn't legal advice. Always check the current rules where you sell.

**The numbers.** Most new sellers underprice. You need your real cost per bar: every oil, the lye, fragrance, packaging and labels. Then you need a price that covers your time and still leaves a profit, whether you sell wholesale, at markets or on Etsy.
